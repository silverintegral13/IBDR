Our question

Can arbitration make Shariah-compliant contracts both enforceable and predictable?

One contract, two sources of authority
Good morning. We're Group 2, and our topic is Shariah compliance and arbitral award enforcement. Let's start with a simple question. If a contract follows Islamic law, and one side refuses to pay, can the other side actually collect? That depends on two things: whether the contract's Shariah promise survives a dispute, and whether a court will enforce the result. So our question is: can arbitration make these contracts both enforceable and predictable? To answer it, we'll follow one deal from signing to payment.

Why it matters

  1. Every deal is governed twice.The contract names a national law, but investors, boards and counterparties also judge it by Shariah.

  2. Disputes live in the gap.No global sovereign reconciles the two, so the forum the parties choose decides which rulebook wins.

Here's the deal. [Click the boxes] A Gulf bank wants to finance a factory for a company in India. The bank can only invest in Shariah-compliant products, so a board of Islamic scholars approves the structure. But the bank also wants a court it trusts, so the contract is written under English law. So from day one, this deal answers to two rulebooks: English law and Shariah. And this is not rare. Islamic finance is a 4.4 trillion dollar market, and most cross-border deals look like this. The problem is simple: when the two rulebooks disagree, who decides?

CaseDubai Court of Cassation, 2025: Islamic banks may not charge late-payment interest, even as “compensation”.

What it teaches

  1. Substance beats labels.Calling a late fee “compensation” didn’t save it: courts ask what a charge does.

  2. Public order overrides consent.A clause both sides signed is still void, and courts strike it out unasked.

  3. Form invites challenge.A murabaha behaves like a loan, so add-on charges get attacked as riba.

First, how does the deal make money without interest? Shariah bans three things: riba, which is interest; gharar, too much uncertainty; and maysir, gambling. [Click Conventional loan] A normal bank would lend 100 and take back 110. That extra 10 is interest. [Click Murabaha] So instead, our bank buys the machinery for 100 and sells it to the Indian company for 110, paid in instalments. The 10 is profit on a sale, so it's allowed. Economically it looks like a loan, but legally it's a sale. Keep that in mind, because that thin line is where disputes start.
Problem 1 · Conflict of laws

Courts can’t apply Shariah

CaseShamil Bank v Beximco (2004): a murabaha “subject to” Shariah was read as plain English law.

What it teaches

  1. Courts apply national law only.In litigation the governing law must be a country’s; Shariah, lex mercatoria or religious codes can’t fill that role.

  2. Vague references are invisible.“Subject to Shariah” gives a judge nothing specific to apply; only rules written in by name become terms.

  3. Certainty can cost intent.The bank recovered its money, but the Shariah basis both sides signed up for disappeared from the deal.

Now the company stops paying, and the bank goes to an English court. Look at the clause. [Read it] Subject to Shariah, governed by English law. [Press →] The court asks two questions. Is Shariah the law of a country? No. Does the clause name specific Shariah rules? No. So the court simply ignores Shariah and applies English law. This actually happened, in Shamil Bank in 2004. The bank gets paid, but the Shariah basis both sides signed up for disappears. So going to court protects the money, but not the deal's Shariah character.
Problem 2 · The non-compliance defence

Shariah as an exit door

CaseDana Gas (2017): the issuer called its own US$700m sukuk non-compliant; the English-law promise was upheld. Course: separability, Kompetenz-Kompetenz.

What it teaches

  1. Distress turns compliance into a weapon.When cash runs short, a borrower will attack even its own deal’s Shariah basis to avoid paying.

  2. A deal is as strong as its weakest law.Split across UAE and English law, the English-law promise held while the local-law parts stayed exposed.

  3. Close the exit in writing.Waive the defence and make each side rely on its own Shariah advice; a DIFC appeal court upheld this in 2023.

Now flip it. [Press →] The company says: our own contract isn't Shariah-compliant, so it's void, and we owe nothing. That sounds absurd, but Dana Gas tried exactly this in 2017 on a 700 million dollar Islamic bond. [Press →] The English-law parts held, because English law doesn't care about compliance. The parts under UAE law were at real risk. So Shariah can protect a deal, or it can become an exit door for a party that doesn't want to pay. Either way, courts leave us with a problem: Shariah is either ignored or used as a weapon.
The way out · Party autonomy

Arbitration can honour Shariah

How much Shariah intent survives?

Fully. The tribunal decides under the rules the parties chose, not just a country’s law.

CaseSanghi Polyesters (England, 2000): the court refused to disturb an ICC award where Shariah was to prevail. Course: the arbitrator is a creation of the contract.

What it teaches

  1. Party autonomy is the bridge.A court can’t make Shariah the governing law, but a tribunal can, because the parties chose those rules.

  2. Courts defer to the choice, not the creed.The award stood because the contract said Shariah prevails on conflict, not because the court endorsed Shariah.

  3. Expertise is part of the bargain.Parties can appoint Shariah-literate arbitrators; Malaysia’s 2026 rules even let tribunals consult a Shariah council.

This is where arbitration comes in. A judge applies the law of the state. An arbitrator applies the rules the parties chose. That's party autonomy. [Click down the ladder] In court, Shariah is ignored or reduced to ordinary contract terms. In arbitration, the parties can choose Shariah as the rules that govern the deal, and appoint arbitrators who understand it. Arbitration also closes the exit door. Because of separability and Kompetenz-Kompetenz, if the company claims the whole contract is void, that argument goes to the tribunal, not to a friendlier court. So arbitration solves both problems: the deal keeps its Shariah character, and nobody escapes it easily.

But winning an award is only half the job. If the company still doesn't pay, the bank has to go to a court where the company has assets and ask it to enforce the award. Under the New York Convention, that court can refuse if the award goes against its public policy. And public policy is different everywhere. [Click through the four countries] Say the award is 100 of price and profit, plus 12 as a late fee. In Saudi Arabia, the 12 is cut as interest. In Dubai's financial centre, it's unclear. Malaysia allows it only as real compensation. In Vienna, all 112 gets paid. Same award, different results. So the real test is enforcement. And in 2025, a court in Vienna faced exactly that test.

Two businessmen in Austria had a commercial dispute. Their contract said a named arbitrator would decide it under Islamic law, based on what is fair. In January 2024, the arbitrator awarded about one million euros. The losing side didn't challenge the award, but didn't pay either. So the winner went to the Austrian courts to enforce 320,000 euros of it. The courts agreed, and in May 2025 the appeal court confirmed it. Then the media found the case, there was public anger, and a major Austrian party called for banning such clauses. The parties were both in Austria, but the question the court faced is the one every Shariah award faces when it reaches an enforcing court.
The case · Why it is an international question

What was at stake

The legal question

Issue 3 · CertaintyCertainty

Domestic facts, international law

  1. Austria's law is the Model Law.s. 603 ZPO copies Art. 28(1): “rules of law”, as in 80+ countries.

  2. Same kind of test as the NY Convention.It judged the result, as Art. V(2)(b) does.

  3. Vienna is an international seat.A ban limits what foreign parties can agree.

  4. In India it would have to be international.Only these may use non-national rules: s. 28(1)(b).

And that question is: can a secular court enforce an award decided under religious rules? It comes down to three issues. Can parties choose religious rules instead of a country's law? When a court checks the award, does it look at where the rules came from, or at the result? And if scholars disagree about Islamic law, is the award too uncertain to enforce? Austria's arbitration law follows the same UN Model Law as India and over 80 other countries, so the answer here matters well beyond Austria.

Our view

  1. The enforcing court decides the remedy.The contract sets the rules, but the court where the assets sit decides what is actually paid.

  2. Public policy is dynamic.Austria enforced this award in 2025 and is now debating a ban: a safe seat today can change tomorrow.

  3. Test the result, not the source.If courts rejected awards for where their rules came from, no award under non-national rules could travel.

Notice what has happened across our story. Saudi Arabia used public policy to cut interest out of an award. Vienna used public policy and found nothing to cut. Same tool, opposite directions, but the same method: judge the result, not the religion behind it. We think that's the right method, because if courts rejected awards just for using religious rules, no such award could ever travel. But public policy also moves. Austria enforced in 2025, and a year later politicians want a ban. A safe place to enforce today may not be safe tomorrow.

Five learnings for deal teams

Back to our question

Can arbitration make Shariah-compliant contracts both enforceable and predictable?

EnforceableYes

If Shariah is chosen as the rules and the result passes public policy.

PredictableOnly by design

So back to our deal. What should the bank and the Indian company have done? [Tick each] One: draft for where you'll enforce, which means knowing where the assets are. Two: if Shariah matters, name the specific rules, because vague references bind no one. Three: close the exit door at signing, with both sides confirming the deal is compliant and waiving that defence. Four: design remedies courts will accept, like compensation for actual loss instead of interest. Five: choose the forum and the arbitrators carefully. And our answer? Arbitration can make these contracts enforceable, if Shariah is chosen as the rules and the result passes public policy. Predictable? Only if you plan for it, because public policy changes from country to country and over time.
